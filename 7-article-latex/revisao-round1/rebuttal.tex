\noindent The authors would like to thank the reviewer for spending his/her time to assess the manuscript and for the constructive and informative comments provided in the review. According to the reviewer's comments, the revisions of the current manuscript are as follows:

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent \textbf{1. Comment:} Protocol of vehicular and ad hoc network is not same but in this work is not taking it that way.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. Vehicular networks such as IEEE 802.11p/DSRC and C-V2X differ from general-purpose mobile ad hoc networks in protocol stack, mobility model, and infrastructure assumptions, and the two should not be treated as interchangeable. Our contribution operates strictly at the computation layer of a single node, deciding \emph{when} to spend an inference step, and is agnostic to the underlying communication protocol. The operational-risk state $\ell_t$ it emits is a compact payload that either class of network could disseminate. To make this distinction explicit, a clarification paragraph has been added as the last paragraph of Section~2.1, highlighted in red on page~3. The paragraph states that we do not treat the two protocol families as interchangeable and that protocol-level concerns such as medium access, routing, and dissemination latency are outside the scope of this paper.

~

\noindent \textbf{2. Comment:} What TERA-Gen means in this protocol.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} Thank you for pointing this out. TERA-Gen is the \emph{TERA episode Generator}, the synthetic-data component of the TERA (Temporal Evaluation and Risk Assessment) ecosystem. It generates driving episodes with a deterministic, frame-precise risk onset, calibrated against SHRP2 odds ratios, and this property is what makes frame-level evaluation of the scheduler possible. The acronym is now expanded at its first mention in the Introduction, in the contributions paragraph of Section~1, highlighted in red on page~2. Its full specification remains in Section~3.1.

~

\noindent \textbf{3. Comment:} Conceptual hypothesis investigated by TERA has not been explained well. The informative scheduling signal block is not justified with explanation.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer that the term ``informative scheduling signal'' deserved an explicit definition, and one has been added to the hypothesis paragraph of Section~1, highlighted in red on page~2. In short, an informative scheduling signal is an uncertainty trajectory whose level separates episode phases, staying low and stable during redundant intervals and rising ahead of and during risk transitions. This is the property depicted in the central block of Figure~1. Without it the gate has no temporal basis on which to discriminate, and with it thresholding entropy becomes a meaningful allocation criterion. The caption of Figure~1, on page~3, has also been extended in red to explain the block in the same terms.

~

\noindent \textbf{4. Comment:} Sensor stream of Operational architecture of TERA need to be explained.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. An explanatory passage has been added to the opening of Section~3, right after the description of the predictive core, highlighted in red on page~6. The passage explains that the sensor stream at the input of Figure~2 is the frame-synchronous concatenation of the four on-board modality groups detailed in Section~3.1, namely behavioral-visual channels from the cabin-facing detector, kinematic channels (speed, longitudinal accelerations, gyroscope), driving-context channels (road type, precipitation, illumination, traffic density), and acoustic event channels, plus two detector-reliability indicators, which together yield $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{31}$ per frame at approximately 9.6\,fps. It also clarifies that at each timestep the MHEG gate inspects only the kinematic variation $\Delta x_t$ and the buffered entropy $H(p_{t-1})$, and that the full vector reaches the recurrent core only when the gate fires.

~

\noindent \textbf{5. Comment:} Efficient inference cycle of the MHEG runtime computation scheduler algorithm need to be explained for its complexity.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. A complexity analysis paragraph has been added immediately after Algorithm~1 in Section~3.4, highlighted in red on page~9. The gate itself (lines 2--4 and 8--10) costs $\mathcal{O}(D)$ and $\mathcal{O}(1)$ per timestep, since it performs only a maximum over channel differences, one closed-form binary entropy, and three threshold comparisons, with no matrix operation. The recurrent update of line~5 costs $\mathcal{O}(4(DH{+}H^2))$ and executes only when the disjunction fires, so the expected per-frame cost is $c_{\mathrm{gate}} + (1-s)\,\mathcal{O}(4(DH{+}H^2))$, where $s$ is the suppression rate. This analysis is consistent with the component-level complexity profile already reported in Table~4.

~

\noindent \textbf{6. Comment:} Frame-by-frame temporal evidence for seed 42 graph is not explained well.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. A detailed reading guide for Figure~5 has been added to Section~4.2, in the paragraph that follows the first mention of the figure, highlighted in red on page~12. It explains each visual element of the figure, namely the probability curve, the entropy curve, the onset line, the detection marker, and the suppressed spans, and it walks the reader through the AF-TOI+MHEG and Baseline columns for the progressive and abrupt episodes of seed~42. Under AF-TOI the entropy is low and flat through the redundant pre-onset stretch, which is suppressed, then rises as the transition approaches, forces dense computation around $t_0$, and detection follows within a few frames. In the Baseline column the entropy stays flat, so suppression lands indiscriminately, including inside the onset window.

\noindent The authors would like to thank the reviewer for spending his/her time to assess the manuscript and for the detailed and constructive comments, which certainly contributed to improving this work.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{General comment:} This manuscript presented a framework for trustworthy selective computation in resource-constrained vehicular edge inference, the topic looks interesting, but I do have quite a few major and minor concerns. My comments are as follows:

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We thank the reviewer for the interest in the topic and for the detailed reading. Each concern is addressed individually below, and the corresponding changes are highlighted in red in the revised manuscript.

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent \textbf{1. Comment:} In the abstract and introduction sections, the background, motivation, and novelty are not clear. For instance, the main methodological contribution is AF-TOI, which transfers temporal structural information from a non-causal reference model to a causal deployment model, with the objective of organizing the predictive uncertainty around risk transitions. The motivation is interesting, but the methodological distinction from existing methods to temporal knowledge distillation, sequence-level knowledge transfer, temporal representation learning, uncertainty-aware inference, and adaptive/selective computation is not sufficiently clear. The authors should provide a much more explicit discussion of what is fundamentally new in AF-TOI.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer, and an explicit novelty discussion has been added as the closing paragraph of Section~2.6, highlighted in red on pages~4 and~5. Temporal knowledge distillation and sequence-level transfer optimize agreement with a teacher's \emph{predictions}, spread the alignment signal uniformly over time, and pursue accuracy or compression as the objective. AF-TOI instead optimizes the \emph{shape of the uncertainty trajectory} around a deterministic onset, through the asymmetric temporal weighting of Eq.~6 anchored to $t_0$, so the training target is entropy stratification across episode phases rather than fidelity to teacher outputs. Uncertainty-aware inference methods consume uncertainty at runtime but do not reshape its temporal structure during training, and adaptive or selective computation methods assume an informative confidence signal rather than inducing one. To the best of our knowledge, using onset-anchored structural alignment to \emph{create} the scheduling signal that a selective-computation gate later consumes is the specific contribution of AF-TOI. The rewritten abstract also states this argument more directly.

~

\noindent \textbf{2. Comment:} In the related work section, more your own comments on state-of-the-art methods are suggested, which would facilitate the problem formulation.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. Beyond the new comparative discussion added to Section~2.6, described in our response to Comment~1 and highlighted in red on pages~4 and~5, each subsection of Section~2 closes with our own critical reading of the corresponding line of work, for example the reliance of exit and skip criteria on instantaneous confidence in Section~2.4 and the marginal character of probabilistic calibration in Section~2.5. Table~1 consolidates this commentary by marking, for each work, which properties required by trustworthy selective computation are present, partial, or absent. The new red paragraph in Section~2.6 makes the resulting problem formulation explicit.

~

\noindent \textbf{3. Comment:} The main experimental conclusions are obtained using TERA-Gen, where the risk onset is deterministically defined at frame level. This design is useful for isolating the proposed mechanism and for avoiding annotation ambiguity, but it also creates a highly controlled experimental environment that may favor the proposed onset-oriented training strategy. The authors do not establish whether the observed temporal organization of uncertainty and the resulting scheduling benefits persist under realistic driving conditions, where risk transitions may be noisy, gradual, abrupt, ambiguous, or inconsistently annotated.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree that this is the central external-validity question, and we addressed it on three fronts. First, the revised limitations in Section~5 now state the concern explicitly, in a new paragraph highlighted in red on page~24, acknowledging that a controlled environment could in principle favor an onset-oriented training strategy. Second, we point to the design elements that mitigate, without eliminating, this risk. Episodes mix progressive and abrupt regimes in fixed proportion (Section~3.1), Movement~6 evaluates recipe families never seen in training, and the stress campaign of Section~4.8 spans nine onset and noise scenarios, including degraded entropy, lagged entropy signals, missing kinematic channels, and false onsets, which are precisely the noisy and ambiguous conditions the reviewer describes, within the limits of a synthetic environment. Third, the manuscript already commits to a concrete real-data protocol on DADA2000 in Section~5, whose frame-level abnormal-onset annotations provide a real, frame-precise onset, and the protocol specifies transfer regimes, split hygiene, re-calibration rules, and success criteria. We prefer to report real-data results in a dedicated study rather than as a rushed appendix, and the paper's claims are scoped accordingly.

~

\noindent \textbf{4. Comment:} A central claim of the study is that temporal organization of uncertainty is important for safe selective computation, but the current experiments appear to be strongly tied to the risk-transition patterns defined by the experimental setup. The authors should investigate whether AF-TOI generalizes to unseen temporal risk trajectories, different onset durations, abrupt versus gradual transitions, intermediate-risk conditions, changes in the distribution of input features, or distribution shifts between training and testing conditions.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We thank the reviewer for the detailed list, and we note that several of the requested axes are already evaluated in the manuscript. The revision now makes this mapping explicit in the red paragraph of Section~5 on page~24. Abrupt versus gradual transitions are stratified in Table~10, and the miss-rate benefit concentrates exactly in the abrupt regime. Intermediate-risk conditions unseen in training are the object of Movement~6, whose borderline recipes A01--A03 and L01--L03 were generated independently and never used for training. Onset-timing perturbations are covered by the entropy-lag sensitivity study of Table~18, which quantifies the lag budget each regime tolerates. Degraded input conditions are covered by the high-noise, missing-kinematics, and false-onset scenarios of the stress campaign in Section~4.8. What the current study cannot establish is generalization under real distribution shift between training and test. This is stated in red in the limitations and is the object of the planned DADA2000 protocol, described in our response to Comment~3.

~

\noindent \textbf{5. Comment:} The experiments are not sufficient to support the proposed method. The study should include stronger baseline comparisons, particularly with representative methods based on confidence-based selective inference, entropy-based scheduling without AF-TOI, temporal smoothing or temporal filtering, adaptive computation/early-exit mechanisms where applicable, and existing uncertainty-aware resource allocation strategies.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree that broader baseline coverage strengthens the study, and the revised manuscript adds a dedicated baseline experiment in the new Section~4.11 and Table~19, highlighted in red on pages~21 and~22. It covers the two families that were not yet explicitly instantiated, a \textbf{confidence-threshold gate} and a \textbf{smoothed-entropy gate} with a moving average of window $w{=}5$, both without the kinematic channel, simulated on the stored full-inference trajectories of the factorial study (seeds 42--45) under oracle-calibrated thresholds that favor the baselines. The result is clear-cut. Purely confidence or entropy driven gates deadlock on a buffered low-entropy value and can safely suppress at most $0$--$2\%$ of frames. When tuned to spend the same computation as the entropy plus kinematics gate, they miss $99\%$ of critical episodes on unorganized trajectories and $59\%$ on organized ones, whereas the entropy plus kinematics gate sustains $\mathrm{FR}=0$ at $96\%$ and $69\%$ suppression, with an effective delay of $0.96$\,s on organized versus $1.90$\,s on unorganized trajectories. The remaining requested families map onto experiments already present in the manuscript, as detailed below.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response to item i) entropy-based scheduling without AF-TOI.} This baseline is already the centerpiece of the factorial design. The Baseline+MHEG configuration in Table~8 applies exactly the same entropy plus kinematics scheduler to trajectories without AF-TOI organization, and it is the negative control on which the paper's main argument rests, with the miss rate unchanged and $\mathrm{TTD}_{\mathrm{ef}}$ worsened by 13.9\% in the twelve-seed campaign.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response to item ii) confidence-based selective inference.} The post-hoc calibration study in Section~4.7 and Table~14 evaluates three confidence-rescaled variants, namely temperature scaling, Platt scaling, and isotonic calibration, each combined with the scheduler and re-calibrated per seed. These are precisely selective-inference baselines driven by rescaled confidence, and they show that improving confidence calibration does not reproduce the effect of temporal organization.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response to item iii) temporal smoothing or filtering and other allocation heuristics.} An explicit smoothed-entropy gate, built as a moving average over the buffered entropy with window $w{=}5$, is now evaluated in the new Section~4.11 and Table~19. Smoothing does not repair the deadlock of purely entropic gating, and at matched suppression it inherits the same coverage collapse as the raw confidence gate. This complements the pre-existing cost-matched campaign of Section~4.8 and Table~17, where MHEG is compared at identical compute budget, within $\pm 2\%$, against random, unstructured-aggressive, and lagged allocators. The lagged control is the budget-matched equivalent of a low-pass filtered scheduling signal, and the entropy-lag study of Table~18 quantifies the lag effect parametrically. At equal cost, all structure-free controls become unsafe near onset, reaching up to 44\% FS@onset and collapsing coverage to 22--24\% on rare transients.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response to item iv) early-exit mechanisms.} Multi-exit and early-exit architectures act on the \emph{depth} axis of a forward pass and presuppose a deep backbone with intermediate heads. Our deployed model is a compact two-layer recurrent network of approximately 58K parameters in which the scheduling decision acts on the \emph{time} axis, that is, on whether to run the step at all. A depth-wise early-exit baseline is therefore not directly applicable to this architecture without changing the model class itself, so we rely on Section~2.4 to position early-exit methods and to state this applicability boundary. We consider a systematic comparison against multi-exit backbones on the same protocol a valuable dedicated study, and it is listed among the future directions.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We respectfully note that the revised manuscript contains a fairly extensive ablation suite, and the revision now cross-references it more visibly. The $2{\times}2$ factorial isolates each component (Table~8), the $\alpha$ sensitivity of the onset weighting is ablated in Table~11, the miss-penalty parameter in Table~12, and the non-causal reference topology, BiLSTM versus Transformer together with the causal target class, in Table~15. The kinematic channel is removed in the \texttt{kinematic\_signal\_missing} scenario of Section~4.8, and the entropic signal is degraded and lagged parametrically in Tables~17--18. Together these ablate every component of the chain, from the generator and the induction weighting to the reference topology, the kinematic trigger, and the entropic gate. In addition, the factorial core on which these ablations rest was re-run with twelve seeds in this revision, as described in our response to Reviewer~2, Comment~5, so the central contrasts now carry exact-test significance rather than descriptive effect sizes. Should the reviewer have a specific additional ablation in mind, we would be glad to include it.

~

\noindent \textbf{7. Comment:} The authors uses the term `trustworthy' relatively broadly, but the current experiments mainly demonstrate improvements in computational efficiency and detection delay under a controlled experimental setting.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. An explicit operational definition has been added as the opening paragraph of Section~2.2, highlighted in red on page~3. It states that throughout the paper the term is used in a restricted, operational sense, under which a selective-computation policy is trustworthy when it preserves critical-episode coverage ($\mathrm{FR} \le 0.05$) while suppressing computation, under the controlled conditions evaluated here. It also states that no claim is made regarding broader dimensions of trustworthiness, such as security, privacy, or adversarial robustness, which are out of scope.

~

\noindent \textbf{8. Comment:} The study reports an attractive model size and recurrent memory footprint, and estimates compatibility with several embedded/automotive platforms, but these results are based on parameter counts, memory requirements, and computational complexity rather than actual execution on physical embedded hardware.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. This is stated as the fourth limitation in Section~5, and the revision reinforces it at the point of use in Section~4.9, in a sentence highlighted in red on page~22, where we now state that all embedded-viability figures in that subsection are analytical, based on parameter counts, memory footprints, and FLOP counts, or measured on a reference GPU, and that none derives from execution on physical automotive hardware. Characterization on STM32H7, Cortex-M55, and SA8155P class platforms is part of our immediate future work.

~

\noindent \textbf{9. Comment:} The authors discuss vehicular edge networks, but the actual evaluation is primarily performed at the single-node inference level. The study does not experimentally investigate multi-node coordination, communication overhead, offloading, or network-level resource allocation.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer, and this boundary is drawn explicitly in the manuscript. An empirical multi-node study is listed among the immediate priorities.

\noindent \textbf{10. Comment:} The authors acknowledge that the current study is limited to a controlled synthetic setting and a single embedded node, and that naturalistic data, physical automotive hardware, distributional robustness, sensor faults, adversarial perturbations, and multi-node studies remain future work. These limitations are important and should be reflected consistently throughout the study.

\vspace{2mm}
