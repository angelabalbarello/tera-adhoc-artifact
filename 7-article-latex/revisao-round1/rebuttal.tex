\noindent The authors would like to thank the reviewer for spending his/her time to assess the manuscript and for the constructive and informative comments provided in the review. According to the reviewer's comments, the revisions of the current manuscript are as follows:

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent \textbf{1. Comment:} The protocols of vehicular networks and ad hoc networks are not the same, but in this work this distinction is not considered.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer that vehicular networks (e.g., IEEE 802.11p/DSRC, C-V2X) and general-purpose mobile ad hoc networks differ in protocol stack, mobility model, and infrastructure assumptions, and that they should not be treated as interchangeable. Our contribution operates strictly at the \emph{computation layer of a single node} -- deciding \emph{when} to spend an inference step -- and is agnostic to the underlying communication protocol; the operational-risk state $\ell_t$ it emits is a compact payload that either class of network could disseminate. To make this distinction and the scope explicit, a clarification paragraph has been added at the end of Section~2.1 (highlighted in red): ``A clarification on scope is warranted: vehicular networks (e.g., IEEE 802.11p/DSRC, C-V2X) and general-purpose mobile ad hoc networks differ in protocol stack, mobility model, and infrastructure assumptions, and we do not treat them as interchangeable. (...) Protocol-level concerns such as medium access, routing, and dissemination latency are outside the scope of this paper.''

~

\noindent \textbf{2. Comment:} What does TERA-Gen mean in this protocol?

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} Thank you for pointing this out. TERA-Gen is the \emph{TERA episode Generator}: the synthetic-data component of the TERA (Temporal Evaluation and Risk Assessment) ecosystem. It generates driving episodes with a deterministic, frame-precise risk onset, calibrated against SHRP2 odds ratios, which is the property that makes frame-level evaluation of the scheduler possible. The acronym is now expanded at its first mention in the Introduction (Section~1, contributions paragraph, highlighted in red): ``TERA-Gen (the \emph{TERA episode Generator}, the synthetic-data component of the ecosystem) generates synthetic episodes with a deterministic onset (...)''. Its full specification remains in Section~3.1.

~

\noindent \textbf{3. Comment:} The conceptual hypothesis investigated by TERA was not well explained; the informative scheduling signal block lacks adequate justification or explanation.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer that the term ``informative scheduling signal'' deserved an explicit definition. The following text has been added to the hypothesis paragraph of Section~1 (highlighted in red): ``By an \emph{informative scheduling signal} we mean an uncertainty trajectory whose level separates episode phases: low and stable during redundant intervals, rising ahead of and during risk transitions. This is the property depicted in the central block of Figure~1: without it, the gate has no temporal basis on which to discriminate; with it, thresholding entropy becomes a meaningful allocation criterion.'' The caption of Figure~1 has also been extended (in red) to explain the block in the same terms.

~

\noindent \textbf{4. Comment:} The sensor stream of the TERA operational architecture needs to be explained.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. An explanatory passage has been added to the opening of Section~3, right after the description of the predictive core (highlighted in red): ``The sensor stream at the input of Figure~2 is the frame-synchronous concatenation of the four on-board modality groups detailed in Section~3.1: behavioral-visual channels from the cabin-facing detector, kinematic channels (speed, longitudinal accelerations, gyroscope), driving-context channels (road type, precipitation, illumination, traffic density), and acoustic event channels, plus two detector-reliability indicators -- yielding $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{31}$ per frame at $\approx$9.6\,fps. At each timestep the MHEG gate inspects only the kinematic variation $\Delta x_t$ and the buffered entropy $H(p_{t-1})$; the full vector reaches the recurrent core only when the gate fires.''

~

\noindent \textbf{5. Comment:} The efficient inference cycle of the MHEG runtime computation scheduler algorithm needs to be explained in terms of its complexity.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. A complexity analysis paragraph has been added immediately after Algorithm~1 (Section~3.4, highlighted in red): the gate itself (lines 2--4 and 8--10) costs $\mathcal{O}(D)$ and $\mathcal{O}(1)$ per timestep (a maximum over channel differences, one closed-form binary entropy, and three threshold comparisons, with no matrix operation), while the recurrent update of line~5 costs $\mathcal{O}(4(DH{+}H^2))$ and executes only when the disjunction fires. The expected per-frame cost is therefore $c_{\mathrm{gate}} + (1-s)\,\mathcal{O}(4(DH{+}H^2))$, where $s$ is the suppression rate. This analysis is consistent with the component-level complexity profile already reported in Table~4.

~

\noindent \textbf{6. Comment:} The frame-by-frame temporal evidence for the seed-42 graph is not well explained.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. A detailed reading guide for Figure~4 has been added to Section~4.2 (Movement~1, highlighted in red), explaining each visual element (probability curve, entropy curve, onset line, detection marker, and suppressed spans) and walking the reader through the AF-TOI+MHEG and Baseline columns for both the progressive and the abrupt episodes of seed~42: entropy is low and flat through the redundant pre-onset stretch (suppressed), rises as the transition approaches, forces dense computation around $t_0$, and detection follows within a few frames; in the Baseline column, flat entropy makes suppression land indiscriminately, including inside the onset window.

\noindent \underline{Response to Reviewer \#3:}
\end{large}

\vspace{10pt}

\noindent The authors would like to thank the reviewer for spending his/her time to assess the manuscript and for the detailed and constructive comments, which certainly contributed to improving this work. According to the reviewer's comments, the revisions of the current manuscript are as follows:

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent \textbf{1. Comment:} In the abstract and introduction, the context, motivation, and originality are not clear. The main methodological contribution is AF-TOI, which transfers temporal structural information from a non-causal reference model to a causal deployment model, aiming to organize predictive uncertainty around risk transitions. The motivation is interesting, but the methodological distinction from existing methods -- such as temporal knowledge distillation, sequence-level knowledge transfer, temporal representation learning, uncertainty-aware inference, and adaptive/selective computation -- is not sufficiently clear. The authors should provide a much more explicit discussion of what is fundamentally new in AF-TOI.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer, and an explicit novelty discussion has been added at the end of Section~2.6 (highlighted in red). In summary: temporal knowledge distillation and sequence-level transfer optimize agreement with a teacher's \emph{predictions}, spreading the alignment signal uniformly over time, with accuracy or compression as the objective; AF-TOI instead optimizes the \emph{shape of the uncertainty trajectory} around a deterministic onset, through asymmetric temporal weighting (Eq.~6) anchored to $t_0$, so the training target is entropy stratification across episode phases rather than fidelity to teacher outputs. Uncertainty-aware inference methods consume uncertainty at runtime but do not reshape its temporal structure during training, and adaptive/selective computation methods assume an informative confidence signal rather than inducing one. To the best of our knowledge, using onset-anchored structural alignment to \emph{create} the scheduling signal that a selective-computation gate later consumes is the specific contribution of AF-TOI. The rewritten abstract also states the argument more directly.

~

\noindent \textbf{2. Comment:} In the related work section, it is suggested to include more of the authors' own commentary on the state-of-the-art methods, which would facilitate the problem formulation.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer. Beyond the new comparative discussion added to Section~2.6 (see Response to Comment~1), we note that each subsection of Section~2 closes with our own critical reading of the corresponding line of work (e.g., the reliance of exit/skip criteria on instantaneous confidence in Section~2.4, and the marginal character of probabilistic calibration in Section~2.5), and Table~1 consolidates this commentary by marking, per work, which properties required by trustworthy selective computation are present, partial, or absent. The new red paragraph in Section~2.6 makes the resulting problem formulation explicit.

~

\noindent \textbf{3. Comment:} The main experimental conclusions are obtained using TERA-Gen, where risk onset is deterministically defined at frame level. This design is useful to isolate the proposed mechanism and avoid annotation ambiguity, but it also creates a highly controlled environment that may favor the proposed onset-oriented training strategy. The authors do not establish whether the observed temporal organization of uncertainty and the resulting scheduling benefits persist under real driving conditions, where risk transitions can be noisy, gradual, abrupt, ambiguous, or inconsistently annotated.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer that this is the central external-validity question, and we have addressed it on three fronts. \textbf{(i)} The revised limitations (Section~5, highlighted in red) now state the concern explicitly: ``A related caveat is that a controlled environment could, in principle, favor an onset-oriented training strategy.'' \textbf{(ii)} We point out the design elements that mitigate, without eliminating, this risk: episodes mix progressive and abrupt regimes in fixed proportion (Section~3.1); Movement~6 evaluates recipe families never seen in training; and the stress campaign of Section~4.8 spans nine onset/noise scenarios, including degraded entropy, lagged entropy signals, missing kinematic channels, and false onsets -- precisely the noisy, ambiguous conditions the reviewer describes, within the limits of a synthetic environment. \textbf{(iii)} The manuscript already commits to a concrete real-data protocol (Section~5, ``Planned real-data validation'') on DADA2000, whose frame-level abnormal-onset annotations provide a real, frame-precise onset; the protocol specifies transfer regimes, split hygiene, re-calibration rules, and success criteria. We prefer to report real-data results in a dedicated study rather than as a rushed appendix, and the paper's claims are scoped accordingly.

~

\noindent \textbf{4. Comment:} A central claim of the study is that the temporal organization of uncertainty matters for safe selective computation; however, the current experiments appear strongly tied to the risk-transition patterns defined by the experimental setup. The authors should investigate whether AF-TOI generalizes to previously unseen temporal risk trajectories, different onset durations, abrupt versus gradual transitions, intermediate-risk conditions, changes in input feature distribution, or distribution shifts between training and test conditions.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We thank the reviewer for the detailed list, and we note that several of the requested axes are already evaluated in the manuscript; the revision now makes this mapping explicit (Section~5, red paragraph). Specifically: \textbf{(a)} abrupt versus gradual transitions are stratified in Table~10, with the miss-rate benefit concentrated exactly in the abrupt regime; \textbf{(b)} intermediate-risk conditions unseen in training are the object of Movement~6 (borderline recipes A01--A03 and L01--L03, generated independently and never used for training); \textbf{(c)} onset-timing perturbations are covered by the entropy-lag sensitivity study (Table~18), which quantifies the lag budget each regime tolerates; \textbf{(d)} degraded input conditions are covered by the high-noise, missing-kinematics, and false-onset scenarios of the stress campaign (Section~4.8). What the current study cannot establish is generalization under \emph{real} distribution shift between training and test; this is stated in red in the limitations and is the object of the planned DADA2000 protocol (see Response to Comment~3).

~

\noindent \textbf{5. Comment:} The experiments are not sufficient to substantiate the proposed method. The study should include more robust baselines, particularly representative methods based on confidence-based selective inference, entropy-based scheduling without AF-TOI, temporal smoothing or filtering, adaptive computation/early-exit mechanisms when applicable, and existing uncertainty-aware resource allocation strategies.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We agree with the reviewer that a broader baseline coverage strengthens the study, and the revised manuscript now \emph{adds a dedicated baseline experiment} (new Section~4.11 and Table~19, highlighted in red) covering the two families that were not yet explicitly instantiated: a \textbf{confidence-threshold gate} and a \textbf{smoothed-entropy gate} (moving average, $w{=}5$), both without the kinematic channel, simulated on the stored full-inference trajectories of the factorial study (seeds 42--45) under oracle-calibrated thresholds that favor the baselines. The result is clear-cut: purely confidence/entropy-driven gates deadlock on a buffered low-entropy value and can safely suppress at most $0$--$2\%$ of frames; when tuned to spend the same computation as the entropy+kinematic gate, they miss $99\%$ of critical episodes on unorganized trajectories and $59\%$ on organized ones, whereas the entropy+kinematic gate sustains $\mathrm{FR}=0$ at $96\%$/$69\%$ suppression, with effective delay $0.96$\,s on organized versus $1.90$\,s on unorganized trajectories. The remaining requested families map onto experiments already present in the manuscript, as detailed below.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response to item i) entropy-based scheduling without AF-TOI:} This baseline is already the centerpiece of the factorial design: the Baseline+MHEG configuration (Table~8) applies exactly the same entropy-plus-kinematics scheduler to trajectories without AF-TOI organization, and it is the negative control on which the paper's main argument rests (miss rate unchanged, $\mathrm{TTD}_{\mathrm{ef}}$ worsened by 11.8\%).

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response to item ii) confidence-based selective inference:} The post-hoc calibration study (Section~4.7, Table~14) evaluates three confidence-rescaled variants -- temperature scaling, Platt scaling, and isotonic calibration -- each combined with the scheduler and re-calibrated per seed. These are precisely selective-inference baselines driven by (rescaled) confidence, and they show that improving confidence calibration does not reproduce the effect of temporal organization.

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response to item iii) temporal smoothing/filtering and other allocation heuristics:} An explicit smoothed-entropy gate (moving average over the buffered entropy, $w{=}5$) is now evaluated in the new Section~4.11 (Table~19): smoothing does not repair the deadlock of purely entropic gating, and at matched suppression it inherits the same coverage collapse as the raw confidence gate. This complements the pre-existing cost-matched campaign of Section~4.8 (Table~17), where MHEG is compared at \emph{identical compute budget} ($\pm 2\%$) against random, unstructured-aggressive, and \emph{lagged} allocators -- the lagged control being the budget-matched equivalent of a low-pass (filtered) scheduling signal -- and the entropy-lag study (Table~18) quantifies the lag effect parametrically. At equal cost, all structure-free controls become unsafe near onset (up to 44\% FS@onset; coverage collapse to 22--24\% on rare transients).

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response to item iv) early-exit mechanisms:} Multi-exit/early-exit architectures act on the \emph{depth} axis of a forward pass and presuppose a deep backbone with intermediate heads; our deployed model is a compact two-layer recurrent network ($\approx$58K parameters) in which the scheduling decision acts on the \emph{time} axis (whether to run the step at all). A depth-wise early-exit baseline is therefore not directly applicable to this architecture without changing the model class itself; we now rely on Section~2.4 to position early-exit methods and state this applicability boundary. We consider a systematic comparison against multi-exit backbones on the same protocol a valuable dedicated study, and it is listed among the future directions.

Should the reviewer have a specific additional ablation in mind, we would be glad to include it.

\vspace{2mm}

\noindent \textbf{11. Comment:} The authors should comprehensively discuss both the limitations of the proposed method and future directions, for example, how to further improve performance by integrating other learning paradigms. \vspace{2mm}
\noindent \textbf{Response:} We thank the reviewer for the suggestion.
